--- date: '2026-11-10' title: 'Navier–Stokes: Physics of Fluid Motion' tags: [math, science] ---

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how liquids and gases move. Developed by Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes in the 19th century, they apply conservation of mass and Newton's second law to a small parcel of fluid, accounting for pressure, viscosity and external forces such as gravity.

They are a continuum model: fluids are treated as smooth matter, not as individual molecules. That assumption fails at small scales (see Limits).

The equations

For an incompressible, Newtonian fluid with constant density and viscosity:

ρ(∂u/∂t + (u·∇)u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + ρg
∇·u = 0
  • ρ: density
  • u: velocity field
  • t: time
  • p: pressure
  • μ: dynamic viscosity
  • g: external acceleration (e.g. gravity)
  • ∇ (nabla): the vector of spatial derivatives (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z), explained below

The ∇ operator

∇ ("nabla" or "del") is a vector of partial derivatives, (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z). On its own it means nothing; it becomes a concrete operation once applied to a field. The equations use it in four ways:

  • Gradient ∇p: applied to a scalar field, it yields a vector pointing in the direction of steepest increase. For pressure, −∇p therefore points from high to low pressure.
  • Divergence ∇·u = ∂ux/∂x + ∂uy/∂y + ∂uz/∂z: a scalar measuring how much a vector field spreads out of (positive) or flows into (negative) a point. Zero means no net source or sink.
  • Advection (u·∇)u: the operator u·∇ = ux∂/∂x + uy∂/∂y + uz∂/∂z is the rate of change along the flow direction. Applied to u, it says how the velocity changes as the fluid carries itself along.
  • Laplacian ∇²u = ∇·(∇u) = ∂²u/∂x² + ∂²u/∂y² + ∂²u/∂z²: compares the value at a point with the average of its surroundings. It is positive where the field is lower than its neighbours, so it smooths differences out (diffusion).

Momentum equation

  • Inertia ρ(∂tu + (u·∇)u): the acceleration of a fluid parcel. The first term is the local change of velocity at a fixed point, the second is the convective change as the parcel moves into regions of different velocity.
  • Pressure −∇p: pushes fluid from high towards low pressure.
  • Viscosity μ∇²u: diffuses momentum between neighbouring layers and smooths out velocity differences.
  • Body forces ρg: external forces such as gravity.

Continuity equation

∇·u = 0 states that incompressible fluid is neither created nor destroyed locally: what flows into a region flows out. For compressible fluids the general form lets density vary:

∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρu) = 0

What viscosity does

The viscous term μ∇²u acts like diffusion of momentum: neighbouring layers of fluid drag on each other, so sharp differences in speed get smoothed out. Think of a fast stream running alongside a slow one. In the simplest case, with flow only along one direction, the term reduces to the heat equation, ∂u/∂t = ν ∂²u/∂y², with kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ.

Seen from the average flow, the top layers move right and the bottom layers move left, with a sharp edge between them. Viscosity makes neighbouring layers drag on each other, so the motion fades away from the edge outwards until everything moves together. Solved numerically with a finite-difference scheme.

Applications

  • Aerospace: lift, drag, pressure distributions and boundary layers, used to design efficient and stable aircraft.
  • Weather and climate: numerical weather prediction solves forms of the equations coupled with thermodynamics and moisture; climate models use them for atmospheric and ocean circulation.
  • Civil and mechanical engineering: pipe pressure losses, pumps, turbines, HVAC, flow around bridges, dams and channels.
  • Biomedicine: blood flow, pressure variations and the effect of vessel narrowing. Blood is non-Newtonian, so this needs additional constitutive models.
  • Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD): analytical solutions rarely exist for realistic geometries, so CFD discretises the domain into cells and solves numerically for velocity, pressure, temperature, turbulence and forces, replacing many expensive physical experiments.

Connection to graph theory

A pipe network is a directed graph: junctions, reservoirs and pumps are vertices, pipes are edges carrying a flow qe. Maximizing throughput from a source S to a sink T is the classical max-flow problem. Navier–Stokes enters through the edge capacities.

Conservation: incidence matrix as discrete divergence

Flow conservation at every internal vertex reads Bq = 0, where B is the graph's incidence matrix. This is the discrete counterpart of ∇·u = 0. The link is exact in finite-volume methods: the divergence theorem turns mass conservation over a cell Ω into a sum of face fluxes,

∮∂Ω u·n dS = 0  ⟶  Σe∈∂Ω qe = 0

so a CFD mesh is itself a graph with cells as vertices and faces as edges. Discrete exterior calculus formalizes this correspondence.

Capacity: physics instead of a fixed number

Max-flow assumes fixed capacities 0 ≤ qe ≤ ce. In a fluid network, flow depends on pressure, geometry, viscosity and flow regime. For steady laminar flow in a cylindrical pipe, the Hagen–Poiseuille equation (derived from Navier–Stokes) gives:

Q = (π R4 / 8μL) · ΔP

Flow scales with R4, so small changes in radius dominate capacity. It also mirrors Ohm's law, with ΔP as voltage, Q as current and 8μL / (πR4) as resistance, which is why pipe networks can be solved like electrical circuits (Kirchhoff's laws).

What does not carry over

Max-flow is a combinatorial problem. The full fluid problem is continuous, nonlinear and constrained by momentum transport, viscosity and turbulence, none of which graph flow captures. Optimizing outflow under the full equations is PDE-constrained optimization:

maxu,p,θ Qout   s.t.   Navier–Stokes, ∇·u = 0, boundary and design constraints

where θ are design variables such as pipe diameters, topology or pump settings. Graph models suit water distribution, vascular networks, industrial piping and microfluidics. An open question is whether max-flow solutions can initialize or approximate the optimal design of a Navier–Stokes-governed network, and whether flows on ever finer graphs converge to the continuous optimal-control problem.

Limits

Continuum assumption

The continuum model holds when the flow's length scale L is much larger than the molecular mean free path λ. In micro- and nanochannels or rarefied gases this no longer holds. The Knudsen number quantifies it:

Kn = λ / L
  • Kn < 0.001: continuum mechanics is valid.
  • 0.001 < Kn < 0.1: slip-flow effects matter; the no-slip boundary condition becomes unreliable.
  • 0.1 < Kn < 10: transition regime, requires kinetic modelling.
  • Kn > 10: free-molecular flow.

Gas molecules travel a random distance (on average λ) before colliding with each other, and bounce off the channel walls. Shrink the channel or lengthen the mean free path and the walls take over from the neighbours.

When the molecular scale matters, alternatives are:

  • Molecular Dynamics (MD): simulates individual atoms and their forces; suited to nanoscale transport.
  • Boltzmann equation: describes the statistical distribution of molecular velocities and links molecular motion to macroscopic flow. Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) solves it for rarefied gases.

Turbulence

Turbulent flow has fluctuations across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. The equations describe it, but resolving it is extremely expensive. Engineering practice trades cost against accuracy:

  • Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS): models only the averaged flow; cheapest.
  • Large Eddy Simulation (LES): resolves large eddies, models small ones.
  • Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS): resolves all scales; only feasible for small, low-Reynolds-number problems.

Open mathematical problem

For 3D incompressible flow, it is unknown whether smooth initial conditions always yield smooth solutions for all time, or whether singularities can form in finite time. This is one of the Clay Institute's Millennium Prize Problems.

OpenAI claims its AI produced a proof, formalized in Lean, that finite-time singularities do form under the problem's permitted conditions. It has not yet passed independent scrutiny or been recognized by the Clay Institute, so it remains a claim, not an accepted solution. See also Are LLMs Solving Math?

Conclusion

A compact set of equations covers a vast range of phenomena, from airflow over wings to blood in arteries. Knowing where they stop applying (molecular scales, rarefied gases) and where they are merely hard to solve (turbulence, global regularity) matters as much as knowing how to use them.
